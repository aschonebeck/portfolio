How do people understand energy, cost and climate impact in everyday life?

Energy is abstract and invisible until it needs our attention. A collaboration between KTH, Uppsala University, Ellevio and Bright, funded by Energymyndigheten - the project asked how design can help households navigate power-based tariffs without requiring anyone to become an energy expert.

Rather than treating energy behaviour as a technical problem, it reframed it as a question of perception. Through interviews, workshops, and usability testing with consumers and prosumers (households that produce their own energy, typically via solar panels), I developed and tested concepts of interfaces and ambient objects.

The project ran from November 2021 to July 2022, following a Research by Design methodology, where design is used to test the research question itself.

WHAT WE LEARNED

My contribution focused on user research - facilitated workshops and concept development, translating energy systems into design solutions that fit everyday life rather than demanding changes to it. Key findings from the research:

  • Economic impact is the primary motivator. Climate benefit registers as a welcome side effect, not a primary driver.
  • Activity-specific feedback has the strongest behavioural effect. Users need to see the direct link between actions, consumption and cost.
  • The kWh/kW distinction is consistently misunderstood. Concept framing matters as much as the data itself.
  • Personal historical data is far more motivating than benchmarks against other households.
  • Feedback needs to be timely and contextual to drive action, information presented retrospectively loses its impact.
  • Not all users want to actively monitor their usage. Some prefer energy information as a background signal rather than something to seek out.

Two distinct user groups emerged from the research: active users wanted depth and control — data-driven, curious, willing to engage. Ambient users wanted relevance over detail — so the work split into two distinct directions.

Some users didn't want to depend on a screen for awareness. This led to a second prototype: a lamp that communicates local grid load through light intensity and color. It asked a different question - can energy awareness live in a room without demanding your attention?

Testing the app with 10 participants (5 prosumers, 5 consumers) confirmed the split. Prosumers engaged with the data directly. Consumers responded primarily to real-time price in relation to their own usage.

The lamp was tested differently: room-based, with experience questions after. It sparked curiosity and reflection more than immediate action — as a presence in the periphery.
