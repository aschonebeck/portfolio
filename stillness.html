FIZZLED OUT
— Designing
for Stillness

Stockholm, SWE

Buzz. Ping. Scroll.

Constant stimulation has become the baseline of digital life, not because people want it, but because most systems are built for speed, reaction and availability. Look here, do this, click here, see this. Stillness is treated as something to avoid.

I want to argue the opposite. Stillness as a design choice. Not absence - structure that focuses on what matters. A system designed for stillness holds up even when the user is distracted or tired. It works with you, not against your natural rhythms. Here's what that looks like across three layers of design.

VISUAL PRINCIPLES

Visuals establish expectations before anything is interacted with. Space, color, type, and motion tell users whether a system is demanding or supportive, before they've even read a word.

Enough whitespace creates orientation; it signals what matters without filling every gap. Softer palettes reduce visual strain over time - high contrast has its place, but constant intensity is fatiguing. Typography sets pace: dense, tight fonts accelerate reading while open, broader ones slow it down. And motion should clarify, not compete. Organic transitions feel calmer than those engineered to recapture attention.

INTERACTION DESIGN

Interaction is where cognitive load accumulates most, and where design decisions become felt rather than seen.

Predictable patterns lower effort. When behavior is familiar, attention can rest. Feedback doesn't need to interrupt; subtle confirmation is usually enough. Bounded flows — with clear beginnings and endings — restore a sense of control that endless scrolling removes. And systems can respond to drops in attention rather than pushing harder. Slowing down is sometimes the more considered move.

INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE

Stillness isn't only visual, it lives in the structure itself. A product can look calm and still feel overwhelming if its underlying hierarchy is unclear.

Presence by subtraction means only surfacing what's needed in the moment, keeping focus on what actually matters. Pause-friendly systems let users step away and return without losing their place. And when structure follows an organic logic - one that doesn't demand constant re-orientation - users can move through it without friction.

Designing for stillness isn't about doing less. It's about being more deliberate about what we ask of people.

Attention is finite. When everything demands it, nothing really gets it. When interfaces are clear, people make fewer errors. Quiet design includes more people - those who are tired, distracted, or managing complex lives alongside their screens. And calm communicates confidence: when structure is solid, the interface doesn't need to perform for attention.

Most digital products assume constant availability. Designing for stillness means letting the user move on their own terms. In a world that keeps speeding up, that feels like the least we can do - because no one's at their best when they're all fizzled out.
